# Scriptwriting for animated story / explainer shorts

Measured from 15 top-performing animated shorts by 7 creators (45 s – 2 min, 50k – 30M views).
All example lines below are original illustrations of the patterns; never lift lines from reference videos.

## Hard numbers (what the scripts actually do)

| Metric | Story formats | Comedic explainer | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | 45–78 s (sweet spot ~60 s) | 83–90 s | Shorts reward completion + rewatch; 60 s of story is the max most can hold |
| Word count | 160–250 | 250–300 | ≈ 3.5–3.8 words per second |
| Pace | **195–230 wpm** | 170–215 wpm | Fast narration leaves no room to swipe. Comedy needs micro-pauses for jokes |
| Speech coverage | 95–99 % of runtime | 94–98 % | There is no "breathing room". Longest pause anywhere ≈ 0.7–1.1 s |
| First word | **0.0 s** in every video | 0.0 s | No intro, no logo, no silence. The first frame is the hook |
| After last word | 0.4–1.5 s | 0.6–1.1 s | No outro; the video ends right on the punchline so it loops |
| Avg sentence | 9–13 words (tense scenes: 6) | 10–13 | Short declaratives. Fragments for beats ("Then donuts. Big ones.") |
| Numbers | 0–4 per script | 0–1 (25 in a numbers-driven explainer) | One vivid number beats five accurate ones |

## The five formats found (pick one per video)

### 1. The Twist Tale (the dominant format: 5 of 15 videos, 3–30M views)
A tiny third-person story where someone does something that looks stupid, everyone mocks them, and
a real-world mechanism proves them right. The fact *is* the plot twist.

Beat sheet (percent of runtime):
1. **0–5 % Cold-open paradox.** One sentence stating the strange action *and* its stakes, spoken over the
   most striking image. Formula: *"[Person] did [counter-intuitive thing] — [consequence that seems insane]."*
   e.g. "She paid the thief double to rob her own bank."
2. **5–20 % Setup + rival.** Who, where, what's at stake. Introduce a mocking antagonist or crowd with a
   one-line quoted taunt (dialogue makes it a scene, not a lecture).
3. **20–55 % Pressure builds.** Everyone bets against the hero; social cost rises (laughter, lost money, being
   called crazy). 2–4 escalating beats, each one sentence + one image.
4. **55–70 % Reversal shown, not explained.** The thing happens on screen ("the other track bends like a
   snake"). Viewers who were about to swipe now need the why.
5. **65–85 % "You see," pivot → the mechanism.** In every Twist Tale the explanation starts with *"You see,"*
   (measured position: 66 %, 67 %, 70 %, 75 %, 84 % through). 2–4 sentences of plain-language science/law/history.
6. **Last line: callback that loops.** Echo the cold open with the new meaning, ideally so the last sentence
   flows into the first: e.g. ends "…until she paid him double." → video restarts on "She paid the thief double…"
   Short, rhythmic, often a contrast pair: "Four locks couldn't stop him. One kind word did."

Variant: end on the *reversal* and never say the science out loud — let the visuals carry it (the most-viewed
reference, 30M, does exactly this: the hook line becomes the answer on rewatch, which drives loops).

### 2. The "You" Hypothetical (second-person thought experiment)
"Imagine…" / "What if…" — the viewer *is* the protagonist. 6–20 uses of "you".
1. Hook = the dark/absurd conclusion stated as fact ("When X happens, Y will happen and nobody will notice.")
2. Paradise phase: it's great at first (2–3 beats).
3. Creeping wrongness: small specific details (the emails stop, someone else's photo on your desk).
4. The terrible realisation.
5. End with a direct question to the viewer ("So… would you still step in?") → comments.

### 3. The Escalation Ladder
A single question pushed through ever-more-extreme steps ("How sharp can it get?", "How fast can you spend it?").
Each rung = one sentence + one visual + one sound hit. Rungs get bigger/faster. The final rung flips the
premise ("the best one isn't the sharpest one, it's the one that stays sharp"). Numbers work well here
("23 sports cars every second").

### 4. The Process Reveal
How a thing is made / why it's shaped this way, told as a chronological craft story with one "now comes the
strange part" beat in the middle and a consequence at the end ("every one you've ever seen is one that survived").
Narration often in present tense with a single nameless craftsman ("he").

### 5. The Comedic History Explainer (persona-driven)
Narrator has a personality: casual, jokey, mildly irreverent ("these goofy little fellas", "extremely fabulous",
"but that's a lot of taxpayer money"). Structure: question hook pointing at the visual ("Who's easier to hit,
these guys or these guys?") → problem 1 → solution 1 → problem 2 → solution 2 → ironic modern punchline.
Running gags, rhetorical questions (3–4 per script), and a short sign-off ("Subscribe, please.").
Speech has more short pauses (21–37 pauses > 0.6 s) — these are joke beats; a visual gag lands in each.

## Writing rules (why they work)

- **Hook in ≤ 12 words, no preamble.** The swipe decision happens in ~1.5 s. Never "Did you know" or "In this
  video". State the paradox; the curiosity gap is the hook.
- **Concrete nouns over abstractions.** "a jar of rusty bolts", "a gap the width of a pencil", "salt on the lock".
  Every noun should be drawable — if the animator can't draw it, rewrite it.
- **One image per sentence.** Narration is written to be illustrated. Each sentence = one shot/beat.
- **Dialogue in quotes, max 10 words.** Taunts and one-liners turn exposition into scenes and give the
  animator acting moments.
- **Present tense for stories** (immediacy), past tense only for framing.
- **Specific sensory details** create believability: "his ears turn bright red", "a guy in the corner eating
  crackers loudly".
- **No hedging, no sources on screen.** Say "the story goes" once if it's legend.
- **Mechanism must be simple and true.** Fact-check it. One mechanism per video. The audience will correct you
  in the comments (that's engagement, but a wrong core fact kills trust).
- **End ≠ conclusion.** No "and that's why…", no "thanks for watching". The callback line *is* the end.
- **Read it aloud at 215 wpm.** If you trip, cut words. Target: script word count ≈ duration_s × 3.6.

## Titles (as measured)

Pattern: a curiosity question or paradox, 4–9 words, no clickbait caps (one creator uses a single ALL-CAPS word):
- "Why [common thing] [is/does surprising thing]" — Why Cowboys Never Load Six Bullets
- "The [object] That [breaks a rule]"
- "What If [hypothetical]?"
- "How [superlative] Can a [thing] Get?"
- "He [did impossible thing] With [mundane object]"
Descriptions: either the title again, or 1–2 sentences restating the paradox. Hashtags optional (#history #shorts).
