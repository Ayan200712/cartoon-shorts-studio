# What a top animated-shorts channel does (10 latest shorts, measured Nov 2026)

Source: the ten most recent shorts of one channel in this genre (4.7M–30M views each, one 1-day-old short
excluded from any view comparison). Measured on 480p downloads: shot rate, camera motion, colour, loudness,
scripts from captions, and a frame every 2 s of each video. Motion was judged from measurements and frames,
not by playing the videos. With ten videos this shows what the channel does, not what causes the views.

Use this file when planning shot lists, camera moves, scene mood and endings. The numbers below are targets.

## Visual habits (what to copy as technique)

1. **The camera never rests.** Fly-ins, whip pans, tilts, orbits around a character, slow pushes on talking
   shots. Plan a move per shot (see `editing-and-graphics.md`), and build layers so near things move faster
   than far things (`"layer_space": "parallax"` in assemble.py).
2. **Insert shots: about one in three or four.** Not every shot is a character. Close-ups of the object the
   sentence is about (paint being mixed, gold flakes in a pan, a spool of fuse, a drill), and first-person
   shots (steering wheel, dashcam, looking out of a hatch, a rolling object's view). They reset the eye and
   carry the explanation.
3. **Scenes are lit, not flat.** Shafts of light, haze, warm lamps against cold ambient, and a time-of-day or
   weather shift that follows the story (dusk → night, storm blue, mine gold). Build mood into every set.
4. **Colour is plot.** The dominant colour of a short comes from its topic (pink cell vs grey-blue jail, gold
   mine, storm blue). Pick one colour idea per video and let it dominate; keep a contrast colour for the hero.
5. **Whole-body acting.** Wide shots show full-height characters with legs: walking, pacing, arm-wrestling,
   slapping a hand, tying themselves down. Then smash-cut to a big-face reaction (red glowing eyes, toothy
   grin, shock) for the emotional beat. A head-and-shoulders character alone is not enough.
6. **Props carry the explanation.** In-world text and objects: a newspaper headline, a mine sign, a flashing
   arrow on a tree, a dashcam overlay with speed and date, a paint-colour chart, a scoreboard. Add at least
   two per short.
7. **Effects with intent:** speed lines and motion blur on fast moves, light shafts, particles, cracking
   floors for rage, big explosions for payoffs.
8. **Costume signifier per character:** chef hat, white suit and top hat, sheriff hat, bow tie. The role reads
   from one item.
9. **Endings:** a big payoff gag, often an explosion (3 of 10 end on a mushroom cloud), or a loop where the
   last frame matches the first (the chef pouring oil at both ends). No outro, no call to action.

## Script habits (matches `scriptwriting.md`)

- 10 of 10 open on a person doing something absurd in the first sentence.
- Most give the real-world mechanism a technical name and say it plainly ("salting", "target fixation",
  "flying thunder", "trust the oil"), usually after a "You see," or "Well, it's called…".
- Character quotes in the setup, a rival or crowd that mocks the hero, then an escalation (the sheriff hits
  the stump too), then the payoff.
- 215–232 wpm: about 235–245 words for a 62-second short.

## Sound

- Master at −14.0 LUFS. Music is continuous and ducked under the voice.
- Sound effects are light: roughly one accent per 10 seconds, on impacts and reveals. Don't stack many small
  effects; the voice's pace already fills the soundscape.

## Checklist to apply on every short

- [ ] A shot change every ~2 s; no shot holds a static camera
- [ ] At least one insert or first-person shot in every four shots
- [ ] One dominant colour idea, set by the topic
- [ ] Lit scenes with a mood shift at the twist
- [ ] Wide full-body acting plus at least three big-face reaction shots
- [ ] Two or more explanatory props or in-world texts
- [ ] A payoff gag or loop at the end
- [ ] Sound effects ≤ about 1 per 10 s; −14.0 LUFS
